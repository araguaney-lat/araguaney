Red flags in a donation

Almost every donation is what it looks like: people wanting to help. This guide is for the handful that are not. It does not turn you into an investigator or ask you to judge anyone: it gives you six concrete signals and a three-step protocol that ends where it should, with coordination.

Panel › Help › Red flags

What it protects. In-kind donations can be used to move commercial goods disguised as aid: a company "donates" merchandise and a related party receives it on the other side. That hurts the people who actually need the aid, exposes the center, and can hold up an entire shipment at customs.

The rule that closes it. Donating means transferring ownership irrevocably. The donor does not choose who receives the goods at destination, cannot require their lot to travel together, and has no right to have the goods returned or traced through to delivery. Almost every red flag is a variation of someone trying to break that rule.

What to watch for

The six flags

A single flag does not mean something is wrong. It means it is worth recording and worth a second pair of eyes.

1

A large, uniform commercial lot unrelated to what is needed

Pallets of brand-new product from a single company, all identical, when the needs board is asking for something else. Real aid tends to be varied and responds to what was requested; a full inventory of one SKU looks more like a shipment than a donation.

2

They ask who receives at destination, or want their lot kept together

The clearest flag, because it directly contradicts what the person accepted when donating. Someone donating in good faith wants the aid to arrive; someone who needs their box to reach a particular person is not donating.

3

They ask for delivery paperwork in a third party's name

Proof that "their" goods were delivered to a specific company or person at destination. A donation receipt is issued to whoever donated, never to a third party they designate.

4

Recurring high-value donations with no explanation

Same donor, large volume, again, with no reason that fits (a company with expiring inventory has one; a private individual showing up weekly with pallets does not).

5

They offer to pay for transport in exchange for choosing the route or destination

Money does not buy control of the shipment. A donor may support logistics; what they may not do is direct it.

6

A company refuses to identify itself

A private individual can donate without giving their name, and that is fine — it is the norm. A legal entity cannot, and the system requires it. Refusing to identify, at high volume, is itself the signal.

What to do

Record, escalate, refuse

In that order, and never by confronting the person in front of you.

1

Record

Capture the donation like any other and write what you observed in the notes, as facts: what they said, what they asked for, what they brought. You do not need to label anything. If the system asks you to identify the person because of the volume and they will not, use the exception option and explain there what happened.

2

Escalate

Unusual-volume captures open a review on their own under Reviews, where your coordination or national coordination resolves it. If you saw a flag the system cannot see — a conversation, an insistence — tell your coordinator directly. Whoever captured does not resolve their own review: that is the whole point of escalating.

3

Refuse, if it comes to that

The center has the right not to accept a donation, and owes no justification to whoever brought it. If coordination decides to refuse, the boxes are marked rejected with their reason, and that record stays. Refusing is not accusing anyone: it is an independent operator's decision about its own inventory.

Not your job

Where your responsibility ends

Do not investigate. It is not on you to find out where the goods came from, look up the company, or confront anyone. Observing and recording is all you are asked to do.

Do not decide alone. That is what escalation is for. When in doubt, escalate: one extra review costs nobody anything.

Do not put yourself at risk. If someone insists, pushes, or becomes uncomfortable, do not argue. Accept the donation or say coordination will review it, and report afterwards. Your safety comes before any control.
